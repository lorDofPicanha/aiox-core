# Conclave v1 — Price Psychology Review

## Daniel Kahneman — Chief Decision Scientist

*Reviewing the BRETDA price-exposure decision through prospect theory, anchoring, framing, and System 1 / System 2.*

---

The interesting thing about exposing prices is that you have not made one decision — you have created a sequence of judgments in the buyer's head, and the order in which information arrives determines the answer. Let me point out what is happening psychologically, screen by screen.

A note on what I am judging: not the renders (placeholders), but the **decision architecture** — where the number sits relative to the value, what anchors it, and what reference point the buyer is standing on when they read it.

---

### The central problem: you exposed a number, but you did not build a reference point first

Loss aversion says the buyer evaluates US$8,000–24,000 **relative to a reference point**, not in absolute terms. The same $18,000 is "shockingly expensive" against the reference point *"a pool table"* and "remarkably restrained" against the reference point *"a one-of-a-kind handmade artwork that happens to be playable, exported, made-to-order."*

Right now your site lets System 1 supply the reference point, and System 1 will reach for the cheapest, most available comparison it has stored: a sporting-goods pool table. That is the WYSIATI failure. **The number is exposed before the category is reframed**, so the buyer anchors low and the price reads as a loss.

The decision to expose prices is correct. The *sequencing* of that exposure is where the money is being left on the table.

---

## Per-screen price-psychology scores

### 01 — Home Hero — **8 / 10**
This screen does its job precisely because it shows **no price**. "Brazil's gemstones, made to play" + "Made to order · Brazil" is doing exactly the right work: it is installing the reference point ("gemstone," "made to order," "play") *before* any number arrives. System 1 forms the category here. The hero is the most important price-psychology asset on the entire site and it contains no price — that is correct. The only thing missing is a faint signal of *altitude* (one cue that this is a high-end, exclusive world) so that when the first number lands two screens later it is already framed as "of course." Minor deduction only because the anchor-setting is implicit, not yet deliberate.

### 02 — Collection (flat single-line grid) — **4 / 10**
This is the weakest screen for price psychology, and it would be even if it showed prices. A flat grid of eight equally-weighted tables is an **anchoring vacuum**. Every model carries the same visual weight, so there is no high anchor to make the others feel reasonable, and no low anchor to make the flagship feel earned. When eight options sit at the same altitude, System 1 cannot tell what is the deal and what is the splurge — it defaults to comparing them to *each other* and then to the cheapest external reference it has (again: a generic pool table). Diminishing sensitivity also gets wasted here: the gap between a $9k model and a $24k model should *feel* enormous, and a flat grid flattens exactly that contrast. This layout treats a luxury ladder as a catalog.

### 03 — PDP "Paraíba" (From $XX,XXX, then "Why this object / house / now") — **6 / 10**
Good news and bad news.

The bad: **the price appears before the value is established.** "From [price]" sits high on the page; the three "Why" sections — the actual value construction, the justification, the scarcity — come *below the fold*. So the buyer meets the number with System 1 (cold, fast, "that's a lot for a table") and only *afterwards* does System 2 get the material that would have justified it. You have inverted the order. Justify, *then* price. A price encountered before its rationale is sticker shock; the identical price encountered after its rationale is a reference point.

The good: "Why now" carrying the made-to-order, export-only, limited framing is exactly the loss-aversion lever — *the risk of not being able to have this one* — but it is currently buried where it can no longer rescue the price.

"From $X" framing: **net positive, but fragile.** "From" implies a range and an upgrade path, which is good — it sets a floor, not a ceiling, and primes the buyer that there is something above. But "From" only helps if the buyer can *see what is above it*. On a PDP in isolation, "From $24,500" with nothing visibly more expensive in view just reads as "the price, softened." The word "From" is a promise of a higher anchor that this layout never pays off.

### 04 — Collection 2-band (Atelier / Signature) — **8 / 10**
This is the right instrument. Splitting into **Atelier** (the rare, top, flagship band) above **Signature** (the accessible band) creates the anchoring contrast that screen 02 lacks. The Atelier band sets a *high anchor* — and a high anchor is the single most powerful, cheapest tool you have for making the $8–12k Signature pieces feel reasonable rather than expensive. Diminishing sensitivity now works *for* you: after the eye has rested on the Atelier altitude, the Signature prices land as relief, not as cost. This layout is structurally superior to 02 for one reason: it gives System 1 a reference point you chose, instead of one it scavenged.

The one deduction: the bands must be *legibly* hierarchical in price, not just in grid position. If Atelier and Signature show overlapping or ambiguous prices, the anchor collapses and you are back to a flat grid with two headings.

---

## Overall price-psychology score: **6.5 / 10**

The price-exposure *decision* is sound. The *architecture* around it is half-built: you have the right hero (01) and the right two-band instrument (04), but your default collection page (02) is an anchoring vacuum and your PDP (03) prices before it justifies. The fixable gap is large, which is the optimistic reading.

---

## Verdict: single-line (02) vs 2-band (04) for anchoring

**The 2-band Atelier/Signature layout wins, and it is not close.**

Anchoring is not a nicety here — it is the mechanism that decides whether $18,000 is read as a loss or a reference point. The flat single-line grid (02) provides no anchor, so System 1 imports the worst possible one (commodity pool table) and every price reads as overpriced. The 2-band layout (04) lets *you* set the high anchor (Atelier) so the rest of the line feels earned and the entry pieces feel like restraint.

Make **04 the canonical collection page. Retire 02**, or demote it to a filtered view. A luxury price ladder must be *presented as a ladder*; a flat grid throws away the only free lever you have.

One caution from my own discipline: I am confident in the *direction* (high anchor helps), but you should A/B it, because the *magnitude* of anchoring effects is noisy and I would not trust my intuition — or yours — about how much.

---

## Top 3 concrete framing / anchoring fixes

1. **Invert the PDP order: justify, then price.** Move "Why this object / Why this house / Why now" *above* the price, or at minimum surface a one-line value/scarcity statement adjacent to the number so System 2 has the rationale *before* System 1 reacts. A price is sticker shock before its reason and a reference point after it. Same number, opposite emotion — the only variable is sequence.

2. **Install a deliberate high anchor: name and show a flagship "Atelier" piece at the top of the band, priced at or near the $24k ceiling — ideally one rare made-to-order commission above it.** The most expensive piece in view is doing the most important job on the site: it is the reference point against which every cheaper piece is judged as reasonable. Even a single "from $24,500" Atelier model visible *before* the $9k Signature pieces will compress the perceived cost of the entire line via diminishing sensitivity.

3. **Convert scarcity from decoration into a framed loss.** "Made to order," "export only," "limited" are currently descriptive. Reframe them as *what the buyer stands to lose by waiting* — a loss looms ~2x larger than the equivalent gain. "Each Paraíba is individually commissioned; production slots are limited" reads as a potential loss of access, which is far more motivating than "handcrafted to order," which reads as a feature. This is the lever that lets the price feel like the cost of *not missing out*, not the cost of a table.

---

## Biggest sticker-shock risk (one sentence)

The biggest risk is the **PDP showing "From $XX,XXX" cold, above the fold, before any value or high anchor has been installed** — because at that moment System 1 silently substitutes "a pool table" as the reference point and judges your handmade, export-only artwork a loss, and no amount of justification placed *below* that number can fully undo a first impression that has already anchored.
